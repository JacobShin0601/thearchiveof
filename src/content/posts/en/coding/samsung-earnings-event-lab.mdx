<AnswerBlock question="Can one earnings-day chart tell us why a stock fell?">
  **Not from one day's figures.** A quarterly profit surprise and a share-price return answer different questions. This Lab shows how the pre-announcement price story changes with the observation window, then compares Samsung with the market and a peer. Any remaining explanation is a hypothesis, not an identified cause.
</AnswerBlock>

This page is a companion analysis for [Efficient Markets Through a Bayesian Lens](/en/investing/markets/efficient-markets-bayesian-lens/). That essay used the Samsung announcement to discuss “already priced in” in Bayesian terms; this Lab narrows the question to closing prices, observation windows, and market-relative returns around the event. The result is not a single causal verdict. It is an experiment in matching a claim to the data that can actually test it.

On July 7, 2026, Samsung **guided** to second-quarter revenue of about **₩171 trillion** and operating profit of about **₩89.4 trillion**. The profit figure exceeded the **₩87.3 trillion** LSEG SmartEstimate cited by Reuters by about **₩2.1 trillion**, or **2.4%**. Its shares nevertheless closed at **₩296,000**, down from **₩318,000** the previous session: roughly **−6.9%**. The KOSPI fell about **4.9%**, and SK hynix about **6.1%** that day. [Samsung guidance](https://news.samsung.com/global/samsung-electronics-announces-earnings-guidance-for-second-quarter-2026), [Reuters reporting](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/samsung-flags-19fold-jump-in-profit-but-shares-slump-on-jitters-ai-boom-may-stall-4783723), [historical-close API](https://aikstockdata.com/en/api)

“Profit beat the estimate” and “the shares fell” are both true. The **2.4%** measures one quarter's profit against one estimate. The **6.9%** measures a one-day change in a price for future cash flows and risk. They are not two returns on the same thing with conflicting signs. The word **because** between them requires more evidence.

## “It had already risen”—over which window?

In the **10 trading days** before the announcement, Samsung's close went from **₩353,500 on June 22** to **₩318,000 on July 6**, a decline of about **10.0%**. That hardly looks like a stock steadily climbing into the release. Widen the view to **60 trading days**, though, and the close rose from **₩196,500 on April 7** to **₩318,000 on July 6**, or about **61.8%**. Both are real closes from the same source. [Historical-close API](https://aikstockdata.com/en/api)

Reuters quoted an investor who described strong earnings as largely priced in after an earlier rally. The longer window helps explain that interpretation, but it does not establish that 61.8% was a measurable “pre-pricing effect” of this particular earnings release. Memory prices, AI spending expectations, interest rates, and risk appetite also changed along the way. **Choose the start date after seeing the chart and you can tell opposite stories.** [Reuters report](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/samsung-flags-19fold-jump-in-profit-but-shares-slump-on-jitters-ai-boom-may-stall-4783723)

## A comparator does not decompose the cause

Subtracting the KOSPI's roughly **−4.9%** from Samsung's roughly **−6.9%** yields a simple **−2.0 percentage-point** difference on announcement day. It tells us that the whole market fell sharply. It does **not** mean earnings guidance caused precisely two percentage points of Samsung's decline. Samsung itself is part of the KOSPI, and the stock does not normally move one-for-one with the index.

Switch to SK hynix and the shared semiconductor selloff becomes visible. SK hynix is not a perfect counterfactual for what Samsung would have done without the announcement: the firms differ, and both could react to the same industry and market news.

This chart is a **descriptive starting point for an event study**. Our simple difference is not a formal abnormal-return estimate or a causal effect. A fuller study would estimate normal market sensitivity from earlier data and check for other news in the event window. [Reserve Bank of Australia event-study methodology](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/2004/2004-02/methodology.html)

## Changing an estimate does not change history

The final slider changes **one assumed operating-profit estimate**. It computes:

**Surprise = (announced profit ÷ assumed estimate − 1) × 100**

Using the **₩87.3 trillion** LSEG estimate cited by Reuters gives about **+2.4%**. Assume instead **₩90 trillion**, and the same announcement becomes a roughly **−0.7%** surprise. This is only a thought experiment about one benchmark. It does not make ₩90 trillion the actual historical consensus, or explain the share-price decline on its own. [Reuters/LSEG](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/samsung-flags-19fold-jump-in-profit-but-shares-slump-on-jitters-ai-boom-may-stall-4783723)

Reuters reported elevated prior expectations, worries about the durability of AI spending, and a wider chip selloff; one analyst also pointed to disappointment with revenue. A single profit estimate cannot summarize **everything** investors had priced in. [Reuters reporting](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/samsung-flags-19fold-jump-in-profit-but-shares-slump-on-jitters-ai-boom-may-stall-4783723)

The modest but useful conclusion: **good earnings, a surprise relative to one estimate, and the day's stock return are different measurements.** Changing the window or comparator does not automatically decompose the cause. One episode cannot test how much was priced in or settle the efficient-market hypothesis. That would require a consistently assembled sample of announcement times, pre-announcement estimates, and prices across many quarters.
