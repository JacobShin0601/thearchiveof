## What it calculates

Terminal revenue comes from annual customer cohorts and revenue per customer. Each cohort receives the renewal-and-expansion factor, after which product/subscription and services revenue use separate gross-margin assumptions; platform fixed cost, recurring delivery/support cost, and illustrative partner-distribution cost are then deducted. The channel cost is not a claim about any specific partner agreement.

$$
Exit\ proceeds = \max(Exit\ EV - Net\ debt, 0) \times \frac{Investment}{Pre\text{-}money + Investment} \times (1-Dilution)
$$

The maximum pre-money is the reverse calculation for a target IRR. When terminal equity value is negative or too small, the Lab says the target is infeasible instead of concealing the result at zero. It also places revenue-multiple and EBITDA-multiple exit values side by side. They are not additive: the first remains the return calculation, while the second exposes the profitability expectation embedded in the revenue-multiple case.

## How to use it

1. Pick Bear, Base, or Bull. They are falsifiable assumption bundles, not forecasts.
2. Change product mix and recurring delivery cost. This is where a moat must turn into margin.
3. Read exit EV/revenue and EV/EBITDA together. When EBITDA is negative, the latter is explicitly not meaningful.
4. Change partner distribution cost, dilution, and entry pre-money. A better company is not automatically a better-priced investment.
5. Read the sensitivity table to see what exit condition the productization story actually needs.

## What it excludes

The common-equivalent model excludes liquidation preferences, participation rights, option-pool timing, tax, transaction cost, cash flow, customer concentration, security incidents, and contract-specific terms. “Maximum pre-money” is a reverse-engineering prompt for deeper diligence, not a negotiated term sheet.
