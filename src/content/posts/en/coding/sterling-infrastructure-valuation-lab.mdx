## Why reverse-solving matters

The fact that the data-center market is growing does not prove the stock is cheap. A good business can offer a low expected return if the share price already discounts fast growth and strong margins.

The forward model shows the value produced by selected assumptions. The reverse model changes the question: **To earn 10% annually from today’s price, what EPS must Sterling produce in 2030, and how fast must E-Infrastructure grow to produce it?**

## Model mechanics

The two revenue pools compound separately for four years from their estimated 2026 starting points.

$$
Revenue_{2030}=3.07(1+g_E)^4+1.005(1+g_O)^4
$$

The model applies the selected EBITDA margin, subtracts depreciation, and applies tax to calculate adjusted EPS. Net interest income is held at zero.

$$
Adjusted\ EPS=\frac{(Revenue\times EBITDA\ Margin-Depreciation)(1-Tax)}{Shares}
$$

A bisection solver finds the E-Infrastructure CAGR that makes present value equal the \$563.69 market price. That result is not the market’s single expectation. The same price can be explained by lower growth and a higher multiple, or higher growth and a lower multiple. The sensitivity table makes those trade-offs visible.

## What this model does not answer

- Future acquisitions and their dilution or debt financing
- Different margins for site development and electrical work inside E-Infrastructure
- Customer concentration, project delays, and power-interconnection timing
- The exact timing of backlog conversion into revenue
- The data-center construction cycle beyond 2030

The point is to separate “it grows” from “it is cheap.” Start with the growth required by the current price, then judge whether that number is plausible given Sterling’s capacity and execution.
