As an AI engineer, I often choose between model capability and the cost of completing a task. Frontier models can be valuable for difficult architecture and ambiguous reasoning; a smaller model may suffice for a well-specified implementation or repetitive extraction. The objective is **the total cost of a successfully completed task at an acceptable quality and latency**. Personal experience motivates the question; it does not establish an industry-wide result.

Could older GPUs therefore generate cash for longer? Perhaps. But if newer hardware serves the same work much faster, its higher hourly price can still produce a lower cost per task. **Technical usefulness and attractive investment returns are different claims.** This **second article in the series**, a companion to the [CoreWeave analysis](/en/investing/markets/coreweave-backlog-shareholder-value/) does not assume a conclusion about that company's shares.

The argument follows three questions: does suitable work remain for older GPUs, can they execute it competitively, and can providers recover capital at a price customers will pay?

The [GPU Economics Lab](/en/coding/experiments/gpu-economics-lab/) lets readers change the hypothetical throughput, prices and investment assumptions.

## Four different lives

| Life | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Physical | Does the equipment function? |
| Technical | Can it execute the workload? |
| Economic | Is continued use worth more than sale, replacement or another use? |
| Accounting | Over what period is historical cost allocated? |

Depreciation allocates historical cost. Book value is not the resale price or the present value of future cash flows. Impairment has its own accounting rules. Payback and the economically optimal retirement date are distinct.

## What companies disclose

[CoreWeave's 2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1769628/000176962826000104/crwv-20251231.htm) says it changed its estimated data-center computing-equipment life from five to six years in 2023, reducing that year's expense by \$20 million for the affected equipment. [Amazon's 2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000101872426000004/amzn-20251231.htm) says server lives increased from five to six years in 2024, then a subset of servers and network equipment moved back to five years in 2025. The reported \$1.4 billion 2025 depreciation increase applies to the affected equipment, not GPUs alone.

For a hypothetical \$1 million asset with no residual, straight-line annual depreciation is \$333,333 over three years, \$250,000 over four, \$200,000 over five and approximately \$166,667 over six. An accounting estimate changes reported income; excluding tax and indirect effects it does not itself change the rent a customer pays.

## What model routing does—and does not—show

[RouteLLM at ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/5503a7c69d48a2f86fc00b3dc09de686-Abstract-Conference.html) shows that routing between stronger and weaker models can improve the quality-cost tradeoff under evaluated conditions. Results depend on models, datasets and quality thresholds. A workflow could route planning and difficult review to a frontier model while sending structured extraction to a smaller model, but must count retries, routing overhead, latency and failures.

Lower spend on an external API does not identify the GPU serving it. Infrastructure demand can be linked to hardware choice more directly when an enterprise serves open-weight models itself.

Consider a **hypothetical** enterprise agent workflow. This is a framework for choosing models, not measured operating performance.

| Task | Possible choice | What must be validated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Planning and difficult design | Frontier model | Plan quality and failure risk |
| Document search and extraction | Small or specialized model | Accuracy and cost per task |
| Implementing a specified plan | Model matched to difficulty | Test success and retry cost |
| Verification | Rules or stronger model | Error detection and delay |

Even if smaller-model traffic grows, it does not automatically require older GPUs. Newer hardware can serve the same model faster, and idle capacity also costs money. Routing creates a possible source of demand; it does not establish hardware price competitiveness.

**This single series rises 86% between its observed endpoints.** There are 38 rows across 43 calendar months, with five missing and nine whose snapshot predates the panel month by more than 30 days. A stale catalog is not automatically a wrong quote, but missing months should not be filled as though observed. The dataset also includes effective-date backfills, which are excluded from this example.

This offering’s posted price increased between the two observed endpoints. That does not establish transaction prices or an increase across all A100s. Market supply, provider policies, product configuration and measurement may all matter. The data used here do not identify a causal split between obsolescence and market-wide supply.

## Break-even rent depends on throughput

Assume, purely illustratively, an old GPU delivers 40 tokens per second, a new GPU delivers 120, and the new GPU rents for \$4/hour. At equal quality, input/output composition and latency, the old GPU breaks even on token cost at approximately **\$1.33/hour**. Above that price, the new one is cheaper per token in this simplified example. None of those performance values are measured here.

Cost per million tokens is `1,000,000 × hourly price / (3,600 × effective tokens per second)`. System-level throughput must be matched to system-level cost. Prefill and decode, batching, latency targets and failures matter.

I reviewed [MLPerf Inference v6.0](https://mlcommons.org/2026/04/mlperf-inference-v6-0-results/) but did not establish a directly comparable A100/H100/B200 sample with matching workloads, settings and prices for this draft. That round includes B200 system results for Llama 3.1 8B Offline, but I could not link comparable A100 and H100 submissions under the same conditions to matching rental configurations. I therefore do not rank real GPUs by token cost. Dividing an optimized system submission by its GPU count does not establish the performance of one independently served GPU.

## Customer ceiling and supplier floor

The hypothetical figures answer different questions. The customer comparison assumes matching quality, latency, workload and throughput units; the supplier figure is the initial rent required by the six-year asset model below.

| Price | Meaning | Hypothetical result |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Customer break-even ceiling | Older GPU rent at equal token cost | About \$1.33/hour |
| Supplier zero-NPV floor | Initial on-demand rent recovering new investment and operating costs | About \$3.89/hour |
| Actual contract price | Price agreed by customer and provider | Unknown |

The first is \$4 × 40 ÷ 120. The second solves for the initial rent that makes the asset's discounted cash flows equal its cost. **If the customer's ceiling is below the supplier's required rate, new investment is unattractive under those assumptions.** Effective inference throughput and a provider's paid utilization are different quantities and must not be applied twice.

## The investor faces a different calculation

Consider an illustrative GPU investment of \$30,000 plus \$12,000 in infrastructure, renting at \$3/hour with 70% utilization, \$6,500 annual operating cost and \$1,000 maintenance investment. Assume a 70% renewal probability after three years, renewal pricing at 55% of the initial rate then declining 12% annually, \$3,000 salvage at year six and a 12% discount rate. This **hypothetical** case has an NPV of approximately negative \$16,315. It is not a CoreWeave contract or measured return.

Apply the hypothetical inference break-even rate of \$1.33/hour to the asset model's initial contract and the on-demand NPV falls to approximately **−\$46,871**. A simplified take-or-pay contract with 98% availability for the first three years gives approximately **−\$39,016**, holding the other assumptions fixed. These are not forecast profits: they test whether customer cost competitiveness and supplier capital recovery can coexist under the same hypothetical inputs. Operating and maintenance costs continue even if renewal fails. The initial rate required for zero supplier NPV is approximately \$3.89/hour on demand and \$2.94/hour under the simplified take-or-pay case. Both exceed the hypothetical customer break-even price, so these assumptions leave no price satisfying both conditions. The Lab lets readers link and unlink the price directly.

On-demand revenue depends on paid utilization. A take-or-pay contract may pay for available capacity even when customer utilization is low, subject to contract terms. The simplified model keeps these cases separate and assumes zero tax to isolate operating economics. Physical use, investment payback and the date when continuing operation becomes inferior to sale or replacement are separate questions.

## An installed GPU presents a different decision

The \$3.89/hour figure asks whether a **new purchase** recovers its acquisition cost. After an existing contract ends, the initial outlay is sunk. The relevant comparison is future cash flows and incremental costs versus selling now or another use. That does not erase a loss on the original investment; it simply avoids charging an irreversible past cost a second time when choosing what to do next.

Suppose the illustrative cohort reaches the end of year three. The following is **hypothetical**: three years remain, initial renewal rent is \$1.65/hour, paid utilization 70%, renewal probability 70%, rent declines 12% per year, annual operating and maintenance costs are \$7,500, terminal sale proceeds are \$3,000 and the discount rate is 12%. Costs continue even if renewal fails, and one renewal probability scales revenue across all three years. Taxes, debt, working capital, replacement and alternative-use value are excluded.

| Choice | Value at expiry | Interpretation |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Continue for three years | Approximately −\$682 | Present value of expected future cash flows and terminal proceeds |
| Sell immediately | Assumed \$4,000 | Net proceeds available now |

Selling is preferable in this scenario. Holding the other assumptions fixed, the first-year renewal rate needs to be about **\$2.16/hour** for continuation to equal the assumed \$4,000 sale. At \$2 the continuation value is approximately \$2,541; at \$3 it is approximately \$11,751. This threshold answers a different question from the \$3.89 new-investment figure and changes with the sale value or other opportunities. Neither number guarantees an attractive original return.

The decision also depends on paid utilization. At the assumed \$1.65 renewal price, continuation values are about −\$5,024 at 50% utilization, −\$682 at 70%, and \$3,659 at 90%. Each is below the hypothetical \$4,000 sale, but changing sale proceeds or operating costs can change the conclusion. Changing only the discount rate to 8%, 12% and 16% gives approximately −\$692, −\$682 and −\$671, respectively. Cash flows here have mixed signs and timing, so their sensitivity to the discount rate need not follow one direction.

## Book value and cash flow can move differently

Allocate the same hypothetical \$42,000 initial cost using straight-line depreciation with zero accounting residual. During the first three years the rent is \$3/hour, paid utilization is 70%, and annual operating and maintenance costs are \$7,500, producing \$10,896 of annual pre-tax cash flow. The figures below are year-end values; the annual cash flow excludes initial investment and financing costs.

| Year-end | Book value: three-year life | Book value: six-year life | That year's operating cash flow |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | \$28,000 | \$35,000 | \$10,896 |
| 2 | \$14,000 | \$28,000 | \$10,896 |
| 3 | \$0 | \$21,000 | \$10,896 |

The same asset can have zero or \$21,000 book value at year three depending on the accounting estimate, without that change itself altering the proceeds from sale or renewal. Continuing value can be negative as in the example above, while an asset with zero book value can still generate future cash. Tax, impairment and financing covenants require separate consideration.

## What remains unknown

This limited sample of public prices does not establish a universal decline rate or durable profitability. Contract prices, utilization, power and matched workload benchmarks remain necessary. I do not update the first article's valuation from this sample. Investors need contract expiry, renewal pricing, paid utilization, operating and maintenance costs, and current disposal value. Low renewal prices may still create incremental value for an installed asset, but actual contract and operating data are needed to establish that.

The next step is a longer panel of matched products and genuinely comparable serving benchmarks. **Economic value depends on workload productivity, price, operating cost, contract structure and cost of capital—not age alone.**
