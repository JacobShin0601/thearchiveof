<AnswerBlock question="Is Bloom Energy Corporation (NYSE: BE) a strong AI-power business—and is it a good stock at today’s price?">
  They are different questions. Data-center power scarcity and Bloom’s revenue acceleration are real. Fast onsite generation can be valuable while grid connections and turbine supply remain constrained.

## The next AI bottleneck may be electricity

A GPU does not create value from an unpowered data center. The IEA estimates that U.S. data centers used roughly 180TWh in 2024 and could account for almost half of U.S. electricity-demand growth through 2030. That makes power a real operating constraint, not a speculative slogan.

**Bloom Energy Corporation (NYSE: BE)** sells high-temperature solid-oxide fuel-cell systems, or SOFCs, that produce electricity at the customer site from natural gas, biogas, or hydrogen. They are generators, not batteries. They are also not inherently zero-carbon when run on natural gas. Their proposition is different: for a customer waiting years for a grid connection, the relevant product may be time-to-power rather than the lowest lifetime electricity cost.

That makes Bloom the power-equipment counterpart to the time constraint explored in [Sterling Infrastructure](/en/investing/markets/sterling-infrastructure-fair-value/) and the equity-bridge question in [Applied Digital](/en/investing/markets/applied-digital-corporation-ai-data-center-value/). A bottleneck can create customer value; it does not automatically become lasting shareholder value.

## What Bloom is really selling: time

A 100MW data center that opens twelve months earlier can create real incremental profit. But it does not earn its entire revenue as profit: GPUs, networking, rent, operating cost, and demand all matter. Bloom’s premium is justified only when the incremental contribution profit from earlier operation exceeds the equipment premium and fuel-cost difference.

| Alternative | What the customer buys | Constraint | Implication for Bloom |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Grid connection | Long-run low-cost power | Transmission, substations, permits | Often economic, but timing can be uncertain |
| Large gas turbine | High-capacity generation | Manufacturing lead times, site work | Supply normalization may compress Bloom’s time premium |
| Gas engines | Modular onsite generation | Emissions, noise, installation | Direct onsite-power alternative |
| Bloom SOFC | Dense onsite power | Fuel, stacks, manufacturing capacity | Delivery speed and service economics are decisive |

Experience, installed base, integration know-how and service relationships are genuine assets. None independently prove permanent pricing power. A faster grid buildout, more turbine output, or a credible competing SOFC platform can reduce the value of speed.

## Do not add 2.8GW, \$25B, and 702MW together

Oracle’s April agreement said it intended to procure up to 2.8GW under a master services agreement. Bloom stated that 1.2GW was contracted and deploying. The 1.2GW and the 2.8GW ceiling do not have the same contractual certainty.

Brookfield’s \$25B announcement is a financing framework for AI-infrastructure power projects, not Bloom backlog. Capital provider, project owner, electricity buyer, and equipment supplier each have distinct claims on the economics.

The AEP announcement for a 702MW Google project is similarly easy to misread. AEP plans to invest roughly \$4B and sell power to Google under a 20-year PPA. That is project investment, not necessarily Bloom product revenue; full operation is targeted for late 2028.

The equity bridge is: **announced project scale → contracted equipment capacity → delivered and installed capacity → Bloom revenue recognition → gross profit → FCFF after working capital and CAPEX → diluted common-equity value.**

Every link is contingent. Headline capacity can validate demand, but it does not value the common stock.

## Growth is real; cash-flow quality still needs normalization

Q2 2026 revenue was \$1.065B, up 165.5% year over year, and Bloom’s guidance midpoint is \$4.05B. Product revenue drove the step-up and GAAP operating profit reached \$182.2M. Treating Bloom as the old loss-making fuel-cell story would miss the current numbers.

But first-half operating cash flow of \$300.0M cannot simply be annualized.

Deferred revenue and customer deposits increased by \$301.2M, while accounts payable rose by \$100.4M. Subtracting \$77.8M of PPE additions gives a useful \$222.2M shorthand. It is not, however, a normalized perpetual free-cash-flow run rate.

The amended Q2 10-Q/A also matters. Two customers represented about 44% and 21% of first-half revenue; one unrelated customer represented roughly 73% of Q2 revenue. The filing does not identify the ultimate data-center user. It may be a contractual buyer or financing affiliate. Still, revenue growth is materially more concentrated than a diversified hyperscaler narrative suggests.

## The Oracle warrant is part of contract economics

In May, Oracle exercised its warrant cashlessly and received 1,905,433 shares. Bloom issued another 248,798 shares to induce net settlement. The aggregate fair value of issued shares was \$324.4M.

This is not a claim that the entire amount is a one-quarter expense; the amended filing describes customer-consideration assets and revenue reduction as well. The investment point is economic: large-customer acquisition involved equity-based consideration alongside product capability.

A model should not double-count that cost by subtracting both the entire warrant fair value and all related dilution. The valuation model treats diluted shares separately and uses conservative margins and FCFF assumptions. The larger conclusion remains: headline contracts are not costless transfers to common shareholders.

## A 2030 model that discounts FCFF, not GW

The model starts with 2026 guidance, then builds product, installation, service and electricity revenue separately from delivered GW and installed base. It proceeds through gross profit, operating expense, tax, CAPEX and incremental working capital to FCFF.

| 2030 case | Delivered capacity | Revenue | EBIT margin | Fair value / share |
| :-- | --: | --: | --: | --: |
| Bear | 1.65GW | \$5.0B | 6.4% | \$9 |
| Base | 2.60GW | \$7.6B | 18.8% | \$55 |
| Bull | 4.00GW | \$11.7B | 29.8% | \$165 |
| October 15 close |  |  |  | \$236.30 |

The Base case assumes 2.6GW of 2030 deliveries, 34% product gross margin, 13% operating-expense ratio and a 9.5% discount rate. The Bull case’s 4.0GW and nearly 30% EBIT margin require capacity expansion beyond the announced 2GW figure plus exceptional operating leverage.

Holding Base unit economics and discounting constant, a simple reverse calculation requires roughly **12GW** of 2030 deliveries to reach the reference price. That is not a prediction that the market literally expects 12GW. It is a way to show how much stronger the combined delivery, price, margin, discount-rate, and post-2030-growth assumptions must be.

## What could prove this too conservative?

The strongest contrary case is that Bloom’s speed advantage persists far longer than modeled and becomes a standard data-center design choice. In that world, installed-base service revenue, manufacturing yield, ASP, and margins can rise together. The Oracle, AEP and Brookfield announcements are meaningful evidence for that possibility.

The other side is equally concrete: turbine lead times normalize, grid interconnection improves, competitors improve, large projects slip, stack and warranty costs rise, or financing and dilution consume more of the value than investors assume.

For Q3 and beyond, I will watch product gross margin at higher volume, customer concentration, cash generation after timing effects, actual progress on the contracted Oracle 1.2GW, capacity expansion, and the conversion of options/frameworks into scheduled deliveries.

## Conclusion: a structural winner can still be an expensive stock

Bloom may be a genuine beneficiary of AI power scarcity. Its revenue growth, time-to-power proposition, and initial Oracle contract support that thesis. But the current share price does not merely price the beginning of that story. It prices a long stretch of very high delivery, margin, financing, and competitive success.

A good business can still be a poor entry price. At \$236.30, I see little margin of safety and choose **Sell**—not to reject the power-shortage thesis, but to wait for a price that leaves room for unavoidable execution risks.
